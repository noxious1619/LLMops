Title: The Thirty Years' War and the Peace of Westphalia
Category: Early Modern European History
Version: 1.1

1. Origins and the Defenestration of Prague
The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) was one of the most destructive and complex conflicts in European history, fought primarily within the fractured territories of the Holy Roman Empire. The war originated as a religious conflict between Protestant and Catholic states following the Protestant Reformation, but rapidly evolved into a continental struggle for political hegemony. The immediate catalyst occurred in May 1618 with the Defenestration of Prague, when Protestant Bohemian nobles, enraged by the Catholic Habsburg King Ferdinand’s attempts to curtail their religious liberties, threw three Catholic royal representatives out of a castle window. This act of rebellion sparked a localized conflict that quickly engulfed all of Europe.

2. Phases of the Conflict
Historians generally divide the war into four phases: the Bohemian, Danish, Swedish, and French phases. During the Swedish Phase (1630–1635), King Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden intervened to support the Protestant cause and secure Swedish dominance in the Baltic. Gustavus pioneered modern military tactics, utilizing highly mobile field artillery and disciplined, volley-firing infantry, achieving stunning victories before his death in battle.
The final French Phase (1635–1648) completely stripped the war of its religious veneer. Catholic France, guided by the pragmatic Chief Minister Cardinal Richelieu, openly allied with Protestant powers (Sweden and the Dutch Republic) against the Catholic Habsburgs of Spain and Austria. France’s goal was purely secular: to break the geopolitical encirclement of France by the Habsburg dynasty.

3. Demographic and Economic Devastation
The war was characterized by the widespread use of mercenary armies, such as those commanded by Albrecht von Wallenstein. These vast, poorly supplied armies operated on the principle that "the war must feed the war," meaning they survived by systematically looting, extorting, and terrorizing civilian populations across the German states. Famine and virulent outbreaks of disease, such as bubonic plague and typhus, followed the armies. It is estimated that the Holy Roman Empire lost between 25% and 40% of its total population, leading to a catastrophic economic and agricultural collapse that delayed German unification for over two centuries.

4. The Peace of Westphalia (1648)
Exhausted and financially ruined, the belligerent powers engaged in years of complex diplomatic negotiations in the German cities of Münster and Osnabrück, culminating in the Peace of Westphalia in 1648. The treaties legally recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic from Spain and the Swiss Confederacy from the Holy Roman Empire. The peace reaffirmed the principle of "Cuius regio, eius religio" (whose realm, his religion) established in 1555, but expanded it to include Calvinism, effectively ending large-scale religious warfare in Central Europe.

5. The Concept of Westphalian Sovereignty
The most profound legacy of the treaties was the establishment of the Westphalian system of international relations. The treaties established the principle of state sovereignty, establishing that each nation-state possesses exclusive political and legal authority over its own territory and domestic affairs, free from external interference by foreign powers or supranational authorities like the Pope or the Holy Roman Emperor. This concept of the legally equal, sovereign nation-state remains the foundational architectural principle of modern international law and global diplomacy.
