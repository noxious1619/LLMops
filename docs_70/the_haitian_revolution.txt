Title: The Haitian Revolution and the Abolition of Slavery
Category: Caribbean History and the Age of Revolutions
Version: 1.2

1. The Pearl of the Antilles and the Plantation Economy
On the eve of the French Revolution in 1789, the French Caribbean colony of Saint-Domingue (modern-day Haiti) was the most profitable colony in the world. Often referred to as the "Pearl of the Antilles," it produced roughly 40% of the sugar and 60% of the coffee imported by Europe. This immense wealth was generated entirely through a brutally efficient, high-mortality plantation economy that relied on the labor of nearly 500,000 enslaved Africans, who drastically outnumbered the 30,000 white colonists and 28,000 free people of color (gens de couleur libres). The harsh conditions resulted in a high death rate, requiring constant importation of captives from Africa.

2. Ideological Sparks and the Initial Uprising
The outbreak of the French Revolution and the publication of the "Declaration of the Rights of Man" created profound ideological instability in the colony. White planters sought greater autonomy from France, while free people of color demanded equal citizenship rights. Taking advantage of the fractured colonial elite, the enslaved population initiated a massive, highly coordinated uprising in the northern plains in August 1791, signaling the start of the Haitian Revolution. They systematically burned sugar plantations and killed slave owners, rapidly seizing control of significant territory.

3. The Leadership of Toussaint Louverture
From this chaotic rebellion, François-Dominique Toussaint Louverture, a self-educated former slave, emerged as a brilliant military and political strategist. Toussaint molded the rebel forces into a disciplined, professional army. Navigating a complex geopolitical landscape, he allied with the Spanish against the French, and later switched allegiance back to the French Republic when the Jacobin government officially abolished slavery in 1794. Toussaint successfully expelled invading British forces, who sought to capture the lucrative colony and restore slavery, and eventually established himself as the undisputed political and military ruler of the entire island of Hispaniola.

4. Napoleon's Intervention and Epidemiological Warfare
In 1801, Toussaint issued a constitution that maintained the colony's nominal allegiance to France but declared himself governor for life and abolished slavery eternally. In response, First Consul Napoleon Bonaparte, seeking to reassert absolute French control and secretly intending to reinstate slavery, dispatched a massive expeditionary force of 30,000 veteran troops commanded by his brother-in-law, General Charles Leclerc. Through deception, the French captured Toussaint, who died in a French prison in 1803. 
However, the French military met a devastating enemy: yellow fever. The virus, carried by mosquitoes, decimated the unacclimated European troops. The rebel forces, now led by Toussaint's lieutenant, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, engaged in a scorched-earth campaign, ruthlessly defeating the weakened French army at the decisive Battle of Vertières.

5. Independence and the Global Legacy
On January 1, 1804, Dessalines formally declared the independence of the nation, renaming it Haiti (the indigenous Taíno name for the island). The Haitian Revolution remains the only successful slave revolt in human history that resulted in the foundation of an independent, sovereign state. It represented the most radical assertion of the Enlightenment ideals of liberty and equality. However, the new republic faced severe diplomatic and economic isolation. Fearful of inspiring slave revolts in their own territories, the United States and European powers refused to recognize Haiti for decades. Furthermore, in 1825, France extorted a massive indemnity from Haiti under the threat of naval blockade in exchange for diplomatic recognition, placing a crippling economic burden on the nation that lasted for over a century.
