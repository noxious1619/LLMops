Title: The Soviet Command Economy and the Five-Year Plans
Category: 20th Century Economic History
Version: 1.0

1. The End of the NEP and the Shift to Central Planning
Following the devastation of the Russian Civil War, Vladimir Lenin introduced the New Economic Policy (NEP) in 1921, which allowed a mixed economy with private agriculture and small-scale retail trade to prevent mass starvation. However, following Lenin's death and Joseph Stalin's consolidation of absolute power by 1928, the Soviet state radically changed course. Viewing the NEP as a dangerous ideological compromise with capitalism and fearing military intervention from hostile Western powers, Stalin argued that the Soviet Union was 50 to 100 years behind advanced nations and had to close that gap within ten years. To achieve this, he introduced a rigid Command Economy directed by Gosplan (the State Planning Committee).

2. The First Five-Year Plan and Rapid Industrialization
The core of Stalin's economic strategy was the implementation of the Five-Year Plans. The First Five-Year Plan (1928–1932) focused obsessively on the rapid expansion of heavy industry: coal, iron, steel, electricity, and heavy machinery. The state mobilized the population on a massive scale, utilizing extensive propaganda (such as the Stakhanovite movement, which glorified workers who wildly exceeded production quotas) and the pervasive threat of the secret police. Entire industrial cities, such as Magnitogorsk in the Ural Mountains, were built from scratch in freezing conditions by a mix of enthusiastic young volunteers and millions of forced laborers in the expanding Gulag system. While consumer goods were completely neglected, resulting in severe shortages of basic necessities, the Soviet Union successfully transformed into a major industrial power in less than a decade.

3. Forced Collectivization of Agriculture
To fund this rapid industrialization and feed the growing urban workforce, the state required absolute control over agricultural output. Stalin abolished private land ownership and forced the peasantry onto large, state-controlled collective farms (Kolkhozes) and state farms (Sovkhozes). The state dictated what crops were grown and seized the harvest at artificially low prices, selling the grain abroad to purchase foreign industrial machinery. This policy stripped the peasantry of their independence and tied them functionally to the land, mirroring a return to serfdom.

4. Dekulakization and the Holodomor
Collectivization met with fierce resistance from the peasantry, who slaughtered their livestock and burned their crops rather than surrender them to the state. The Soviet government responded with overwhelming brutality, targeting the "kulaks" (a loosely defined class of slightly wealthier peasants) as class enemies and saboteurs. Hundreds of thousands of kulaks were executed or deported to labor camps in Siberia. The ensuing disruption of agriculture, combined with punitive grain requisitioning quotas enforced by armed state units, led to a catastrophic, man-made famine in 1932–1933. The famine struck the grain-producing regions of the USSR, most devastatingly in Ukraine, where it is known as the Holodomor (death by starvation), resulting in the deaths of an estimated 3.5 to 5 million people.

5. Legacy of the Command Economy
By the outbreak of World War II in 1939, the Soviet Union had achieved Stalin's goal; it had built the heavy industrial base necessary to produce the tanks, aircraft, and artillery that would eventually defeat Nazi Germany. However, the structural flaws of the command economy—its inability to efficiently allocate resources, its reliance on coercion rather than incentives, the stifling of technological innovation outside the military sector, and its chronic inability to produce adequate consumer goods or agricultural surpluses—became permanently embedded in the Soviet system, ultimately contributing heavily to its economic stagnation and collapse in the late 20th century.
